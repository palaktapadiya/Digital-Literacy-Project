Case Study — Cyberbullying (casestudy.md)

What is Cyberbullying?
Cyberbullying is the repeated and deliberate use of digital technology — including social media platforms, messaging applications, online gaming communities, emails, and discussion forums — to harass, threaten, humiliate, manipulate, or intimidate another individual. Unlike traditional face-to-face bullying, cyberbullying is not limited by physical location or time. It can reach the victim at any hour of the day, even within the supposed safety of their own home, making it far more invasive and psychologically damaging than conventional bullying.
How it typically happens — step by step:

The bully identifies a target, usually a classmate, batchmate, or someone from an online community who they perceive as vulnerable, different, or easy to provoke.
The harassment begins privately — through hurtful, offensive, or threatening messages sent via WhatsApp, Instagram Direct Messages, Snapchat, or similar platforms.
The bully may then escalate by creating fake or anonymous social media accounts specifically to post embarrassing photographs, manipulated images, private conversations, or damaging rumours about the victim in public spaces.
Other users, often strangers, begin to join in — liking, sharing, and commenting on the abusive content. This phenomenon is known as mob bullying or pile-on harassment and can spread across multiple platforms within hours.
The victim finds themselves completely overwhelmed, unable to escape the abuse as it follows them across every platform they use — Instagram, Twitter, WhatsApp groups, and even college forums.
In many cases, the victim begins to withdraw from social interactions, stops attending college, and experiences a serious decline in mental health and academic performance.

Who is usually targeted?
Cyberbullying most commonly targets teenagers and young adults between the ages of 13 and 25, making college students a particularly vulnerable group. Individuals who are perceived as introverted, socially different, academically outstanding, or part of a minority group are frequently targeted. Research consistently shows that girls are statistically more likely to experience cyberbullying than boys, though boys are not immune. Students who are new to a college or city and have not yet built a strong social support system are also at higher risk.
Consequences:
The consequences of cyberbullying are wide-ranging, severe, and often long lasting. On a psychological level, victims commonly experience chronic anxiety, depression, low self-esteem, and a persistent sense of helplessness. Academic performance drops significantly as the victim struggles to concentrate, attend classes, or participate in group activities. Social isolation becomes a common coping mechanism, further worsening mental health. In the most extreme and tragic cases, cyberbullying has directly contributed to self-harm and suicide among young people worldwide, including in India. From a legal standpoint, the bully may face serious consequences under Section 66A and Section 67 of India's Information Technology Act, 2000, as well as provisions under the Indian Penal Code related to criminal intimidation and defamation. Institutions may also impose academic disciplinary action including suspension or expulsion.
